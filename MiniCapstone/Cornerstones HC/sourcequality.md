## #sourcequality

### Distinguish between categories and types of information to determine source quality.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

You can’t build a house on shaky foundations, and you can’t support an argument with untrustworthy sources.

Sources of information can be scrutinized in terms of their currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose. One should be able to check how information is vetted and verified and whether there is a means to correct errors. Using this criterion, one can assess the overall credibility of a source and the degree to which it can be used as evidence in one’s work.

You are searching for information about how much sleep you need each night. You ask a friend who seems well-rested how much sleep they get each night, and they say 6 hours. You realize this friend is not a credible source because they have no expertise in sleep science. You then consult Wikipedia, which recommends you get 7-8 hours of sleep each night. You know that Wikipedia is an unreliable source because anyone can edit it. However, you utilize the references on this page and find a blog post from a professor with a Ph.D. in neuroscience specializing in sleep disorders. You check the date on the article, and it was published just a few months ago. The professor is sponsored by the university she works at. The blog post dives into why everyone needs at least 8 hours of sleep for a healthy circadian rhythm. You find this a bit surprising. The source seems credible, but blogs tend to be self-published, so you look into academic research journals for additional publications by this author. From your search, you find an article they published in the New England Journal of Medicine, a reputable peer-reviewed professional journal, and use that article as your primary source. This is a credible source for the information you are looking for, but you continue to look for corroborating sources and counterarguments to increase your confidence, especially if you aim to convince others or present this information in a work product.

***Footnote:*** *You consider various sources - a friend, Wikipedia page, blog post, and peer-reviewed article - when researching how much sleep you need. You determine that the first two sources are unreliable, and although the blog post seems credible, it may be biased as a self-published work. After further searching, you find a peer-reviewed study written by the author and evaluate it based on CRAAP criteria. The article discusses sleep patterns, proving its relevance to your work. The information is current and accurate because the article was published just last year in a reputable peer-reviewed journal. The author has authority based on her credentials in the field, and her purpose is likely to advance knowledge in this field. Based on these factors, you deem the article a high-quality source.*

#### Applying the HC at Minerva

Whenever you work with sources, this HC will aid your vetting process and ensure the final sources you utilize are high-quality and credible. Without trusted sources, evidence is meaningless. Using source quality, you can ensure your evidence has roots in current, relevant, accurate sources with authority and purpose.

Some examples of applications include:

- In a history class, you could apply #sourcequality to analyzing a significant historical event. For example, suppose you are researching how women were impacted by the Great Depression in the United States. In that case, you may utilize a journal written by a woman who lived through the Great Depression and documented her thoughts about the economic disaster. The source will not be current, but that is deliberate because you want to incorporate a direct source from the period. While this woman isn’t a researcher, she is authoritative over her personal experiences, which provides a perspective central to your research questions. Her purpose was to write for herself, and though you cannot check the accuracy of everything she writes, her insights advance your goal of digging deeper into the personal part of the Great Depression. Finally, the journal is relevant since it is a primary source of information from a woman who experienced the Great Depression. Therefore, this source is high-quality and credible for your purposes, even though it does not meet the CRAAP criteria perfectly. You decide you will use this source to back up your analysis. Now you look for sources that provide broader studies of this period and again vet them for source quality.

- For your capstone project, you aim to research the connections between advancing the U.N. Sustainable Development Goals while promoting vegetarianism. You begin by reading a short post written by someone working for the U.N. to get their perspective, but you soon decide to branch out and get outside perspectives, especially since such prioritization could impact policies affecting people in a wide range of countries. You open up ChatGPT and ask it to generate a list of ten credible sources with notes explaining how each source addresses the potential compatibility of vegetarianism to sustainable development, and it generates a list of annotated sources. You begin to look for the sources online and find that six are behind paywalls and you can’t read them, two are accessible using our library systems, and two are non-existent sources. You focus on the two you can access and read and then evaluate the source quality and merits of the arguments in the two sources you can read, following up on promising footnotes you find in those articles to widen the basis of your judgment.

- When participating in academic work, you will often utilize sources. Whether you are using sources to learn something new, support your argument, or brainstorm ideas, you must carefully vet those sources for credibility. However, in everyday life, #sourcequality can be helpful to ensure reliable sources support the information you are absorbing. For example, if you read an article that attempts to argue that vaccines are linked to autism, they will likely reference a dated and inaccurate study that falsely claimed to find such a link. Being a critical reader of potential sources, you check for debunking arguments and quickly come upon a wide range of articles published more recently in scholarly journals that carefully and clearly undermine the reliability of the initial report you found. Consciously applying #sourcequality will reveal that the article’s sources fail the CRAAP criteria and, therefore, the claims in the article are invalidated. This HC gives you the power to fight back against false claims in the media.
