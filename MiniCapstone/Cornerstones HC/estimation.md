## #estimation

### Use estimation and approximation techniques appropriately.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

It all starts on the back of a napkin.

Estimation and approximation techniques are widely applicable across many disciplines. Common techniques to arrive at a quick estimate include using powers of 10, round numbers, and establishing limits, which are minimum and maximum values that the number could reasonably be expected to take. Estimations are especially useful when working with incomplete or inexact information. For example, estimations can be used to conduct a plausibility check when considering quantitative claims as well as to determine the feasibility of an experiment or simulation before embarking on a scientific endeavor.

**Big Question:** How is free choice manipulated?

As a recently-hired advisor to LA’s city hall, you are tasked with planning a revamp to Los Angeles’ tourism sector. To gauge the state of the industry, you want to figure out how many hotels are operating in LA. You never worked in hotels, but an average establishment must have between 20 and 1000 rooms, since a small hotel could have two floors and about 10 rooms on each floor, while large chain hotels could have about 30 rooms distributed across as many floors. You reckon a regular hotel room must house between 1 and 4 visitors, as many people fly in for business trips or take vacations with their families. This means a typical hotel must house between 20 and 4,000 guests at any one time. Since you know the city has around 4 million residents, and you expect hotel guests to be proportional to that number, you assume guests number something between 5% and 10% of the population, fluctuating between seasons. In that case, there are between 200,000 and 400,000 hotel guests to fit into hotels with capacity of between 20 and 4,000. The number of hotels, then, is at least 50 and at most 20,000. These limits are separated by two orders of magnitude, so you take the approximate geometric mean of these bounds. The mean of the coefficients of each limit’s scientific notation is 3.5 and their average power is 2.5. So, you round the power of 10 down to 2 and multiply the coefficient by 3. Finally, you get a grand total of 1,050 hotels in LA. After a quick Google search, you find out the reported number is 1,008. Not bad!

***Footnote:*** *To arrive at a quantity of interest, related figures which are easier to estimate are used progressively, from the number of rooms and the guests they house to the total number of guests in the city. This last figure comes from a potentially problematic assumption, that the number of hotel guests is proportional to LA’s population, but the final estimate is plausible nonetheless.*

- This HC is introduced in the context of Fermi estimates. One conducts Fermi estimates by breaking down the desired quantity into multiple simpler (easier to estimate) quantities, making reasonable estimates about each of the component quantities, and combining them, usually via multiplication, to obtain a final result. This often requries using order-of-magnitude calculations, rather than seeking precise or exact values. Fermi estimates can involve several other estimation strategies to approximate specific quantities in each step.

- This HC can be used broadly for MANY types of estimations and approximations and not just Fermi estimates. It is applicable any time that a student is using quantitative techniques to arrive at a numerical value for a given purpose. The estimation can involve use of measurements, empirical data, observations, analogies, and mathematical approximation techniques.

- This HC is relevant in many different Location Based Assignments and Final Projects across Minerva.

  - For example, some CS LBAs or Final Projects require students to model a system or decision. The estimation may involve measurements, empirical data, observations, analogies, and mathematical approximation techniques. Capturing the relationship between different variables opens plenty of room for estimating either their relative weights (i.e., relative importance), their linear or non-linear behavior (e.g., risk preferences and changes over time), and their relationship to each other (e.g., adding variables to demonstrate independence or multiplying them to show co-dependence).
  - As an NS example, consider this location-based physics assignment described in this Minerva blog post. For this assignment, students are tasked with taking a hike and obtaining various quantities along the way, such as distance, time, heart rate, breathing rate, temperature, and humidity. With these values in hand, students can compute several interesting physical quantities, such as the amount of energy spent to overcome gravity and the amount of sweat evaporated. Estimation and approximation techniques are required to obtain the measurements to begin with, as well as perform the multi-step calculations to get the final results. The precision of the measurements and the impact of inaccuracies on the results must be considered. Additionally, the calculations require various assumptions that can influence the plausibility of the results.

- This HC is applicable when trying to figure out how long a certain task, assignment, or project will take. Various estimation techniques, such as estimation by analogy, can be used to determine how long each subtask will take. For example, suppose a student was trying to estimate how long their Civic Project would take. A project like this requires multiple pieces, including doing research, organizing sources, writing an outline for their research synthesis, completing a project plan, attending meetings, revising their work, writing HC explanations, drafting a presentation, practicing their presentation, writing their final annotated bibliography, etc. For some of these steps, like writing an outline, students might be able to estimate the time required by making an analogy with how long it took them to complete similar tasks for other past assignments. Estimations like this are usually susceptible to the planning fallacy: our tendency to underestimate how long a task will take. Awareness of this bias can help produce more reliable estimates.

- This HC can also be considered when performing dimensional analysis as a quantitative tool for plausibility checking formulas, equations, or calculations, or for deriving the dimension of unknown quantities. This is useful in physics and other sciences.

- This HC can help address questions like “How close is close enough?” for a particular calculation. In some contexts, accuracy is important, but in others, a rough estimation will do. Recognizing this requires applying the principles of this HC.

- It may seem like most quantities are easily searchable nowadays. However, #estimation is still a valued skill in the real world because accurate information and calculation tools are not always readily available, and there are many cases in which the quantity we want to estimate is unknown. For example:

  - In marketing, we need to predict how many customers will use a certain product.
  - In scientific research, we may need to estimate the parameters of a planned experiment or study before embarking on the project, such as the cost of an experiment, the feasibility of detecting a desired signal (its expected strength), and the runtime of a simulation.
  - Job interview questions may even include challenging estimations as a way to assess a candidate’s reasoning and ability to work with limited information. For example, how many spherical balls can you fit into an airplane?
