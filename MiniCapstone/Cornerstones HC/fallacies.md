## #fallacies

### Identify and correct logical fallacies.

So that you can tell exactly why an argument is wrong.

Human beings are not perfect logic machines. Instead, we are prone to making logical errors—and often do so systematically. There are numerous common flawed patterns of reasoning, typically grouped into two large classes: formal fallacies and informal fallacies. Formal fallacies result from errors in an argument’s structure, while informal fallacies contain issues with the content of the propositions in an argument. One can learn to identify and correct the most common sorts of logical fallacies in both inductive and deductive reasoning.

**Unit:** Logical Thinking

**Big Question:** How is free choice manipulated?

A presidential candidate has proposed a new way to provide health care that involves increases in taxation and inclusiveness, but his opponent says that this approach is socialism and eventually will bankrupt the country. In fact, while the proposal does have 'socialist' components, it is complex and nuanced, with many checks-and-balances. The opponent has committed a “straw man fallacy,” attacking a superficial version of the proposal and thus failing to address the proposition in question. Let X be taxation and inclusiveness, Y be socialism, and Z be bankruptcy. In simplified symbolic form, the politician claims that X → Y, that Y → Z and therefore, by hypothetical syllogism, that X → Z. While this argument is valid, it is unsound because the premises X → Y and Y → Z are not necessarily true. While it might be plausible to argue against socialism in health provision, the opponent extremified the proposition to label it ‘socialism’ and refute it, a false premise that makes his argument unsound. The fallacy could have been corrected by arguing only against the specific parts of the proposal: taxation and inclusiveness.

***Footnote:*** *The opponent commits a "straw man" fallacy, an informal fallacy of relevance, as his objection is directed towards a distorted version of the original proposition and thus fails to address the relevant question.*

- You may apply this HC to check yourself on whether you have committed a fallacy or not within your works. For example, if you are evaluating the impact of introducing universal basic income, you may say that “If we introduce a universal basic income, people will feel less of a need to work. It will create a culture of dependence on the government, leading to a complete collapse of our economy”, which is committing the slippery slope fallacy. Knowing #fallacies, you may recognize that there is insufficient evidence to conclude such drastic relationships in the premises of the argument. Correcting this fallacy would require adding more support or evidence to justify that the weak predictions in the argument are actually true, or developing new, more acceptable premises.

- Mastering this HC will allow you to identify fallacies when someone is trying to persuade you regarding areas of media, political discourse, speeches, and debates. For example, When Al Gore was speaking for public engagement on the topic of global warming, he was criticized by his opponents for traveling by private jet ( Meade, n.d.). Having knowledge of #fallacies, you would identify the usage of a “tu quoque” argument.

- When viewing advertisements, knowing #fallacies can help you make informed decisions. For example, a United Van Lines TV commercial (Nikolopaulou, 2023) asked the following questions: *“Is United right for your move? Ask yourself, do you want: a) A seamless professional move? or b) Your possessions set on fire? If you answered A, call United.”* Awareness of false dichotomy fallacies would help you realize that the seemly extreme presentation of these two options is a common marketing ploy. Options (a) and (b) are not so black and white. Perhaps it’s reasonable that you want them both or neither. This flawed premise makes the advertisement’s underlying argument unsound.
