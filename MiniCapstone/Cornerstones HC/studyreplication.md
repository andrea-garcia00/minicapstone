Battling the ongoing replication crisis.

For scientific results to be accepted as well-established they must be reproducible. To determine how broadly applicable the conclusions are, one can replicate the study in a variety of contexts (e.g., different experimental subjects, different scales, different researchers). When the results of an empirical study cannot be replicated, this can indicate that the conclusions drawn are incorrect, that the methods of testing are insufficient, or that an attempted replication may have not accurately replicated the study (e.g., may have conceptually, but not technically replicated the methodology of the study).

**Big Question:** How do humans impact evolution?

You read a summary of a recent research study that concluded that coffee is bad for our health. In another magazine, you read a summary that indicates that coffee is good for our health. You decide to look closely at both studies to see why they came to such different conclusions. The first study found a correlation between coffee drinkers and increased heart disease. This study was done with elderly working-class people in the UK. The second study found a connection between coffee drinking and decreased heart disease. This study was done with medical professionals in Denmark. You conclude that these are not necessarily contradictory, because they are not exact replications of each other. Understanding the potential confounds of using these two restricted samples, you decide to investigate the findings further by replicating the study through an interventional experiment, which would constitute a conceptual replication. You extract from these studies the hypothesis that coffee may be a contributor to heart disease, and a conceptual replication study will help you investigate this claim. You decide to run an RCT in which an intervention group drinks coffee, a placebo group drinks hot water, and a natural history group drinks none of the two (all three groups maintain all other health related habits constant). You find that, on the one hand, the increased metabolism rate due to coffee leads the intervention group to be more active and thereby perform more “cardio” exercise, improving their heart-health. At the same time, coffee seems to increase blood pressure, which poses a risk to older individuals with poor circulation. These findings may provide resolution to the discrepancy between the original studies, and they indicate that your hypothesis must be refined to accommodate the nuance. Nonetheless, because the replication was conducted in a rather homogenous population, you realize there may be problems with generalizing these results more broadly, and you decide that more studies have to be conducted in other populations.

***Footnote:*** *After presenting two seemingly conflicting answers to the question “is coffee bad for your health”, a replication study is suggested to test a more specific hypothesis “is coffee bad for your heart”. The value of such replication is justified, and the results are evaluated in the light of obstacles to generalization, which suggests future replications (conceptual or otherwise) would be required for an adequate exploration of the hypothesis.*

- The application does not explain the relationship between the replication and the original study, including why the type of replication was chosen (i.e., what is the 'value add' of this replication)?

- The application does not consider the validity and/or generalizability of the study replication.

- The application has a major flaw in the study replication design (e.g., doing an exact replication when a partial replication is more suitable).

- The application mistakes a repeating step from a study method as a study replication (e.g., measuring the same variable before and after treatment in an interventional study is NOT a study replication).

- Natural sciences often use study replication to test the validity of studies. For example, in Empirical Analyses, we discussed Blondlot’s N-rays, a new type of radiation that was claimed to be discovered in 1903 (Trentkoff, 2007). Despite the widespread belief that N-rays have medical properties, other physicists have replicated the study and failed to reproduce Blondlot’s results. The replications showed that Blondlot’s claims are false, making the N-rays a form of pseudoscience. Science builds knowledge through study replications.

- The replication crisis is the failure to reproduce the results of many prominent psychological studies (e.g., smiling will make you happier). Thus, when critiquing a paper in psychological courses, it’s important to evaluate its replicability (clarity of study methods) and reproducibility (how likely we would observe the same results).

- The Collaborative Replication & Education Project (CREP) is an open project to encourage study replications in psychological studies, while also addressing the need to replicate highly-cited studies. Collaborators can select one of the listed studies and collect data individually based on the given methods. The collected data will then be analyzed to see if the findings of the dataset agree with each other, which will increase the validity of the existing studies.

- The foundation of science is study replications. Most scientists are engaged in some sort of study replication to confirm and/or extend previous findings.
