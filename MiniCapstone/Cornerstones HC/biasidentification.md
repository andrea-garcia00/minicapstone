## #biasidentification

### Identify and explain how cognitive biases result from psychological mechanisms or use of heuristics.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

An awareness campaign for human cognitive tendencies.

Our understanding of the world depends on what we notice, focus on, and remember. Cognitive biases and emotional states can affect what we notice (or do not notice) and therefore influence our memories and decision making in various ways. For example, people have a tendency to filter out potentially useful information that does not correspond to their preconceived notions. In particular, we tend to seek out information that confirms or is consistent with our prior beliefs, disregard information that is contrary to them, and interpret equivocal information as supporting our beliefs. Heuristics—simple, efficient rules that can make problem solving and decision making faster and easier—can sometimes lead to biases and errors in judgment or reasoning and must be used with caution.

**Big Question:** What does it mean to behave rationally?

You have a relative who is very nervous about a doctor’s appointment, and asks you to go with him. Afterwards, he remembers what the doctor said as much more severe than you do. He was expecting to hear bad news, and thus kept thinking about everything that was even slightly negative, which distorted his memory. Furthermore, you noticed that your relative, instead of listening to the doctor, stared intently at a huge, gleaming syringe and needle that was placed over the counter and was thus subject to a top-down attention bias due to their emotional state. You realise that this attention bias made your relative filter out important, positive information and at the same time worsened their mood. You, on the other hand, took notes—and thus can correct his memory.

***Footnote:*** *A memory bias, working synergistically with a top-down attention bias, resulting from the emotional distress prior to the doctor visit is identified and explained as the cause of the friend's overly-negative memory of the doctor's remarks.*

How to know when to apply #biasidentification, #biasmitigation ([[biasmitigation]]), or #interpretivelens ([[interpretivelens]])?

- Have you identified the relevant cognitive biases?

- Have you identified potential interactions between biases, or between biases and heuristics?

- Have you explained how biases lead to a specific decision/behavior?

- The application miscategorizes a type of bias or identifies an irrelevant bias for the context.

- The application identifies a relevant bias but does not explain how that bias leads to a specific psychological behavior.

- The application identifies multiple biases for a certain context but does not identify the interactions between these biases.

- The application identifies a bias that is not a cognitive bias (e.g., sampling bias).

- In Integrated Learning courses (IL), this HC is classified under the ILOs “Interpersonal Engagement” and “Intercultural Competency.” Cognitive biases shape our everyday behaviors, which may or may not be perceived as rude as we engage with different people and cultures.

- Within computational sciences, we see how decision-making algorithms can be powerful tools to both *mitigate* bias and *promote* bias. On the one hand, using an algorithm can reduce bias by providing an objective and systemitic way to make decisions (e.g., using a machine learning algorithm to build a classification model to help with hiring decisions). On the other hand, algorthims like this can perpetuate cognitive bias, especially if the source training data is problematic. For example, this article discusses the controversy of racial biases in ChatGPT. Such biases reduce the model accuracy, thus it’s important to identify them.

- In EA, you consider the ways in which cognitive biases affect (both positively and negatively) the efficacy of your study habits.

- Recognizing cognitive biases is important in fields where critical decisions are made. The Innocence Project focuses on many cases in which accused persons have been  wrongly imprisoned due to cognitive biases at multiple stages from eyewitnesses to the police and forensic analysts.

- For neurodivergent communities, this HC may be relevant in explaining higher-level behaviors. For example, the role of attentional bias in people with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), or the role of emotional bias in people with major depression disorder. Studying cognitive biases may explain how such neurodivergent people interact in societies. See Ruhe et al. (2019) for an academic study on how people with depression process stimuli with emotional biases.

- The ability to recognize how incidental emotions might affect our decisions and behaviors is a critical “life skill” that can lead to healthier and deeper relationships both personally and professionally.
