## #testability

### Evaluate whether hypotheses lead to testable predictions.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

If it can’t be tested, it falls outside of science.

Scientific thinking requires the ability to identify hypotheses that generate testable predictions. One must learn to identify hypotheses that do not lead to testable predictions and hence cannot be evaluated. One should also be able to generate multiple predictions from a hypothesis and determine how well they will allow us to conclude whether a hypothesis is supported or refuted.

Habit | Empirical Analysis | Thinking Critically | Evaluating Claims

**Class:** Empirical Analysis | Semester One

**Unit:** Scientific Method

**Big Question:** How do we mitigate human-caused climate change?

You work on a local politician's campaign team for the upcoming local elections. You suggest employing more door-to-door campaigning will increase support among residents because they seek more face-to-face interactions. Your colleague argues that while your proposal makes sense, you don't have the means to determine the impact of door-to-door visits. Your colleague is suggesting that your hypothesis that increasing face-to-face interactions will cause an increase in support among residents is untestable. First, you suggest randomizing the intervention (door-to-door campaigning) among the city’s neighborhoods. Then, you inform the other campaign channels to keep the same strategy over the research period. You predict that there will be a larger increase in support among residents in the neighborhoods that received the door-to-door intervention compared to the other campaign channels (control groups). While your hypothesis is testable in principle, however, you still need to find out whether it is testable in practice. You ask your manager how much budget you would be able to allot for this experiment and they give you a sufficient amount to cover all expenses. You then investigate how long your experiment would take and realize you would need to have the results done by the end of the campaign. Since the entire research project would take around 2 months, and the elections are in 4 months time, your hypothesis is determined to be testable in both principle and practice. To improve the testability of the hypothesis even more, you generate a second prediction: that polling numbers will increase significantly in the neighborhoods with door-to-door campaigning compared to the neighborhoods without. Both of these predictions are useful while testing your hypothesis.

***Footnote:*** *Available methods were used to assess the testability of door-to-door visits. Once it was determined to be testable in both principle and practice, multiple predictions were generated that will be used to test the hypothesis.*

### Applying the HC

- Have you generated multiple, independent predictions that would clearly refute or support the hypothesis?

- Have you sufficiently explained and justified your process for generating or deriving predictions?

- Have you identified a prediction that can distinguish between competing hypotheses?

- Have you justified why you focus on a specific prediction?

- Have you considered the practicality of testing the prediction (e.g., budget, duration, ethics)?

- Is your interpretation of the expected results insightful for the purpose of your work?

- Provides only a single prediction.

- Considers the simple negation of a prediction as an additional prediction.

- Provides multiple predictions but they are not connected to the hypothesis or are not specific enough.

- The testability in principle and/or practice is not assessed where needed.

- Fails to identify and critique untestable hypotheses.

- You can use this HC in your business classes to suggest a test of a hypothesis regarding the success of a product, or marketing strategy. Very often, a question will arise regarding the best way to market a product. In this way, you might hypothesize that the best marketing strategy will be social media ads for one month as compared to billboards because social media has the greatest reach. This then leads to clear predictions: (1) a comparison of sales before and after the social media ad campaign should show a statistically significant increase in sales post-campaign. (2) In areas without the social media advertising, sales do not change. (3) One month of only social media ads should lead to greater sales compared to one month of only billboards in regions that are similar demographically. Of course, we should be careful to consider confounding variables!

- This HC is applicable to your research proposal assignments in the Natural Sciences college. For example, your research proposal on solar geoengineering technologies would require a thorough analysis of the testability of your hypothesis given the costly, under-researched, and ethically controversial nature of such technologies.

- You can use #testability to generate and evaluate hypotheses in politics, economics, and other social sciences. Let’s say you want to conduct research to test whether the increase in government investment in public education leads to a decrease in youth unemployment. If the relationship between the variables (investment in education and youth unemployment) is true you would expect (1) states with higher government investment to have higher employment rates of recent graduates, (2) skills provided by improved public education to match with the demands of the job market and (3) long term economic growth (reflected in GDP) due to lower unemployment and high skilled labor. Once you generate multiple predictions, you may test one or more depending on the time, financial, and data constraints.

- The HC can help you assess claims encountered in your daily conversations. Imagine a scenario where your neighbor called to warn you about an unfortunate event foreseen in their dream. Intrigued by the claim, you decided to collect data on all the negative events throughout your day; however, nothing particularly bad happened. When you reported this to your neighbor, they responded by claiming that the future changed because of their warning. It seems that any outcome, whether the predicted event occurred or not, could be interpreted as supporting the claim. Furthermore, even if something negative had happened, the prediction was too broad, meaning that a random event could wrongly be ascribed as a supporting outcome. The inability to falsify makes your neighbor’s claim untestable in principle.

- If your career is in R&D, you will often use #testability to design experiments by selecting to test the prediction that not only supports a hypothesis but also contradicts the alternative ones, making research less costly and time-consuming.
