### Understand and use connotations, tone, and style.

The magic of saying exactly what you mean.

To communicate effectively, one must understand and use connotations—the idea(s) or feeling(s) that the words, tone, and style of a communication imply. Individual words as well as idioms and colloquialisms carry not only literal meanings, but also implied meanings that affect communication.

Habit of Mind | Multimodal Communications | Communicating Effectively | Using Language

**Class:** Multimodal Communications | Semester One

**Unit:** Close Reading

**Big Question:** How does language represent reality?

You are writing a campaign advertisement about police brutality in your hometown in response to recent murders of innocent citizens, and are trying to decide between the words “strong” and “forceful” to describe the police force. The denotative meanings of such words are close enough that in principle they often might be substituted for each other — but their connotations are very different, which cautions against simply using a thesaurus to select words. Because you are trying to raise awareness of unwarranted use of force by police officers, you wish to portray them as aggressive. You therefore choose to go with the word “forceful” because the connotative meaning is slightly more negative, successfully antagonizing the police force for your purpose, as opposed to using “strong”, which might have a positive connotation and portray them as protectors instead of abusers.

***Footnote:*** *The diction chosen to describe the police force was carefully selected to connote a body of unnecessarily aggressive officers (e.g. “forceful”), thus portraying them in a negative light and contributing to the purpose of your campaign as opposed to other word choices that could unintentionally glorify them (e.g. “strong”).*

- When writing a research paper, you should investigate the implications of certain word choices relating to the main concepts of the study. The connotation of the words used can significantly impact whether readers believe the findings of the study to be important, positive, or negative. For example, when the global COVID pandemic spread in 2020, studies began finding that greenhouse gas emissions were decreasing. This was good news for environmental scientists because they could study how when human mobilization decreases, carbon emissions also decrease, thus mitigating global warming. However, communicating these findings required a deep understanding of connotation. For one, you need to be sensitive to the anxieties of people experiencing a global pandemic; wording that holds cheery connotations may come off as tone-deaf. You also must communicate that decreased emissions mitigate but do not cure climate change and that the decreased mobilization of people must be sustained to have lasting effects. Thinking deeply about precisely what you want to say and understanding common misunderstandings allows you to effectively communicate your findings.

- One of Minerva’s seven guiding principles is “Be Selective.” Think for a moment about why that particular term was used. Focusing solely on denotation, we could just as easily have said “Be Picky,” or “Be Choosy.” In fact, if you look up any of the three terms in the dictionary, you will probably see references to the other two. So what are the differences in connotation among *pick*, *choose*, and *select*?

  Let’s start with *pick*. In addition to the meaning that overlaps with choose and select, pick is used to describe the process of collection without discrimination. If you pick fruit, you are not necessarily collecting only the very best fruit. It is also the name of a tool used in mining, which is related to the meaning in “to pick apart.” If you are making specific choices when “picking,” you may be focusing on trivial features. If you say that someone does not have a significant other because they are “picky,” you are implying that they reject people based on traits that should not bother anyone, like that they wore a green shirt that one time. To “nit-pick” is explicitly to object to trivial or insignificant details.

  Contrast that with *choose*. If you are choosing a piece of fruit, you are definitely evaluating multiple pieces and grabbing the best one. Likewise, if you are “choosy,” rather than “picky,” it implies that you have standards. The word “choice” is used as an adjective to refer to items of high quality, particularly in the context of food.

  Finally, consider *select*. If you select a dress to wear, there is no question that you have put thought into the decision. In biology, “natural selection” refers to differential reproduction based on advantageous qualities. Selecting something implies a much higher degree of intentionality than choosing or picking. The adjective “select” is often used to indicate premium versions of products like wine or olive oil. And if someone describes you as “selective” in your dating habits, you have standards, but they are completely legitimate.

  Notice that the shades of meaning between *pick*, *choose*, and *select* are even reinforced by the phonetics of the words themselves. “Pick” is the shortest of the three, with a single syllable consisting of two stops surrounding a short vowel. “Choose” also has a single syllable, but the vowel is longer, and both consonants are continuants. Just as it takes more time to choose an apple than it does to pick an apple, it takes more time to say “choose” than “pick.” Finally, “select” has two syllables, with continuant ‘s’ and ‘l’ sounds. You could also point out that select ends in a stop. Your mouth lingers over the first part of the word, just as you deliberate over your selection, but the word ends with a sense of finality, consistent with your having arrived at the optimal outcome.

- You may use #connotation when faced with different claims in the media. For example, you may see an article that claims: "The widespread adoption of social media has led to a concerning erosion of real-world interactions." In this article, the word "erosion" carries a negative connotation, implying that real-world interactions are being gradually worn away or damaged by the use of social media. The gradual nature of erosion makes it seem like it is a long-term problem, but also an insidious process that can go on without our noticing consciously. The next day, you plan to share the article with a friend, and you notice that they have changed the headline, which now reads “Social media addiction is destroying relationships.” This version of the headline implies a much more urgent and nefarious situation. Having carefully read the article, you conclude that the revised headline misrepresents the actual evidence presented, and you decide not to forward the article, which now seems unnecessarily alarmist.
