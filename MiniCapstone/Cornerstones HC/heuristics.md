## #heuristics

### Identify when to use heuristics and when to avoid them.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

As a rule of thumb, it’s often a good idea to use heuristics.

Heuristics are decision-making processes and strategies that use limited information. They are simple, efficient rules that can make problem solving and decision making faster and easier. They encompass conscious and unconscious strategies that help us take action under uncertainty, generate creative solutions, and quickly categorize or estimate entities. Heuristics do not guarantee correct or useful solutions but can often be efficient and effective tools when time and information are limited. As effective as they can be, heuristics commonly lead to systematic inaccuracies, known as biases. Identifying and understanding heuristics is crucial to fruitfully applying them in appropriate situations and avoiding them when they are likely to lead one astray.

**Big Question:** How can we feed the world?

You work in a neuroscience lab, and your current assignment is to develop a protocol for immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses of human skin tissue. Despite being a common procedure in analyzing mouse skin tissues, a standard protocol has never been developed for the human tissue. You recognized that the existing protocols for mouse tissue may inform the overarching plan, so you decide to use the heuristic of analogical thinking to facilitate problem-solving. Keeping in mind the similarities and differences between human and mice anatomy, you noted three relevant steps that can be adopted from mice protocols to human protocols: tissue extraction, tissue processing, and antibody attachment. Then, you clearly defined the desirable end states for each of the three steps: samples of human tissue stored in 30% sucrose solution, 20-µm cross-sectional slices of human skin tissue, and ultimately the tissue slices with antibodies attached to the proteins of interest. From the third and final state, you work your way backwards to the second final state, so on and so forth until you reach the initial condition. You found multiple pathways from the final state to the initial state, and you tested each of them to identify the strengths and weaknesses of each before demonstrating the protocol to your supervisor.

***Footnote:*** *Given a task without clearly defined steps, two heuristics are utilized to facilitate problem-solving: analogical thinking and working backwards. Guided by thoughtful consideration of superficial similarities, analogical thinking informs the process of creating the overarching outline. Working backwards illuminated multiple possible pathways to reach the goal state from the initial state.*

- In the Decision Theory unit of FA, we learn how to analyze decisions from both an empirical and normative approach. For example, in the context of decision trees, we learn formal tools, such as expected utility, to determine the best sequence of decisions (the best path through the tree). We contrast this with heuristic approaches, such as "maximax" and "maximin," which provide shortcut methods to evaluate how one would make the sequence of decisions under an optimistic or pessimistic mindset. These heuristics are valuable decision analysis tools in the face of uncertainty: when we don't have enough information to quantify all outcomes and probabilities.

- You are researching the benefits and risks of the use of AI technologies in self-driving car systems and you use a Utilitarian ethical framework to defend the claim that these innovations are ethically permissible because, you argue, they result in fewer human deaths than car systems without those technologies. You then explain how this applies the ethical rule of thumb that we should maximize the greatest good for the greatest number.

- We use unconscious heuristics in everyday decisions (e.g., what to eat for lunch, which in-game item to buy). For another instance, when we decide if we should take a personal extension for an assignment, we use the availability heuristic (e.g., estimating how likely you would take an extension this time, based on the most recent instance where you took an extension for another assignment).

- Business startups may have limited information about the current market. Heuristics may be useful when deciding whether or not a business strategy should be implemented.

- You rely on moral heuristics in everyday circumstances to help you make decisions about how to conduct yourself and treat others. For example, you might subscribe to the moral rule of thumb that it’s wrong to steal, and so you wait in line to pay for something, even when the item is inexpensive and the line is long.

- As a voter, if you decide who to vote for based on what they believe about a single key issue, you are using the “take-the-best” heuristic.
