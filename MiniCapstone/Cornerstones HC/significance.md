## #significance

### Apply, interpret, and distinguish practical and statistical significance.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

Is there enough evidence that the pattern you’re seeing really exists in the population? And is its effect big enough to matter?

Measurements drawn from samples are used to make inferences about broader populations, often for the purpose of evaluating whether patterns in the population are “significant.” Tests of statistical significance tell us when a difference or pattern in a sample is likely due to chance or instead reflects strong evidence for a genuine difference or pattern in the population. Setting up, executing, and interpreting the results of significance tests requires one to identify and contemplate the unavoidable statistical errors that are possible. Statistical significance, however, says nothing about whether the size of the difference or pattern matters practically. That is the domain of practical significance, which is evaluated by measuring the effect size. One must take care to distinguish these two very different concepts, using appropriate calculations and interpretations.

You read a journal article that reports on a large clinical trial carried out to compare a new medical treatment for hangover headaches with standard paracetamol. The analysis shows a statistically significant difference in the average duration of a headache when using the new treatment compared to the standard treatment, reported with a standard significance level (p&lt;0.05). This means that, assuming there is zero difference in effect, it is quite unlikely (less than 5% chance) to observe results this extreme or more. However, given the very large sample size, you know that the statistical power is high, so even small practical differences would be deemed statistically significant. Upon closely examining the size of the effect, you note that the decrease in headache duration is only 15 minutes compared to the standard treatment group. Recognizing that a typical hangover headache lasts from 5-12 hours, you become more reserved about the practical implications of the clinical trial. Your reservation is supported by the small Cohen’s d value of 0.1, a standardized measure of practical significance (effect size) when comparing two means, which in this case indicates that the effect of the new treatment is small relative to the existing one.

***Footnote:*** *A measure of significance was interpreted to explain the observed statistical difference considering the sample size, and statistical significance was distinguished from the practical significance of the results.*

- This HC is introduced in Formal Analyses by focusing on t-tests for means and regression coefficients, but there are many other types of tests for statistical significance, and many other measures of practical significance. For example, there are chi-squared tests, ANOVA tests, and several other common tests that are not covered in the Cornerstones. The choice will depend on the variables involved and the purpose of the study. Regardless, all such tools rely on the foundations of #significance.

- In business applications, hypothesis testing may be employed to evaluate the effectiveness of marketing campaigns or strategies. For example, you may test hypotheses about whether there was a significant difference in mean sales across a time period after a new advertisement campaign or compare customer satisfaction levels between two product variations.

- For many natural sciences courses (and some social sciences courses), students are required to read articles published in scientific journals. Knowing the core principles of #significance is often crucial to be able to understand the research methods of the study and evaluate the strength of the conclusions presented by the researchers. In particular, this HC can help you critique the study and the results. For instance, you might read an article that claims to have found multiple p-values less than 0.001. However, it doesn’t discuss practical significance at all! This would lead you to question the practical importance of the observed effect, rather than blindly accepting the conclusions of the paper. You might further notice that no correction was employed to mitigate the effects of doing multiple tests, and thus, the family-wise type I error rate is unacceptably high.

- You’re going to see statistical claims everywhere around you, but you must ask yourself if they are SIGNIFICANT. Applying ﻿#significance﻿ helps us answer questions like…

  - Is this difference "true" (“real”, "genuine") in the population?
  - Can you be confident that this difference would happen again in a repeated scenario?
  - Is this difference big enough to be important?

- Hypothesis testing can be applied across many disciplines. Here are some examples:

  - **Biomedical Research:** Hypothesis testing can be used to evaluate the effectiveness of new drugs or medical treatments. Researchers can set up a hypothesis test to compare patient outcomes in groups with and without treatment. Evaluating both statistical and practical significance is crucial here. For example, it's possible for results to show that a drug does produce a statistically significant difference in improving patient outcomes, however, the improvements might be too tiny to practically matter.
  - **Psychology:** Hypothesis testing is applied to investigate various aspects of human behavior. For example, researchers might use hypothesis tests to test predictions suggesting differences in cognitive abilities between two different groups.
