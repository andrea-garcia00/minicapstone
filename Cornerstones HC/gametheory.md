## #gametheory

### Apply and evaluate game-­theoretic models.

Analyzing the behavior of interacting agents: It’s all fun and games until you realize the implications of the Nash equilibrium!

Game theory provides a framework to study decision-making behavior among multiple interacting intelligent agents, relying on mathematical models of cooperation, competition, and coordination. Formally, a “game” involves agents (“players”), the actions each agent can take (“strategies”), and the “payoffs” for each agent for the various outcomes of the game. The payoffs for a given player depend not only on their own choice of strategy, but also on the actions of other players. An important part of evaluating a game is determining the strategy profiles that lead to an equilibrium, in which no player benefits by unilaterally changing their strategy. Under certain assumptions about the rationality of the players and the information they have access to, these equilibrium strategy sets can be used to predict the outcome of the game, thus providing applications in wide-ranging contexts, such as war, traffic flow, ecology, and marketing.

**Big Question:** What is a good decision? & How can conflicts be resolved?

You own one of two pizza restaurants in a shopping mall. You have found a new gluten-free dough that attracts many customers, but your competitor has an amazing pizza sauce that also generates lots of business. You want to use the sauce, so you ask for the recipe in exchange for your dough recipe. After some hesitation, she says that she will send it to you. However, her hesitation leads you to think that she may not be honest about all the ingredients. In fact, you both have the same decision to make: share the correct recipe or share the incorrect recipe. You create a payoff matrix that shows the outcomes for each combination of strategies that you and your rival may employ, and predict that if both restaurants offer better pizzas, more people overall will choose to go eat pizza at the mall, thereby boosting the profits for both restaurants. However, if both parties are honest, one is always tempted to betray the other for an even higher payoff that comes from having a superior pizza that would monopolize the pizza market. So, if each of you acts in your own self-interest, you should both share incorrect recipes corresponding to the Nash equilibrium of the game. This means that when both of you adopt this strategy (being dishonest), neither shop can unilaterally change their strategy and get a higher payoff. On the other hand, your relationship with this other pizza place is likely to be on-going, and similar decisions may arise repeatedly. You realize that if either of you becomes dishonest at any point in time, the other will likely break relations too and adopt a more defensive strategy. In fear of having that happen, you choose to cooperate and find that the other shop also has this fear, and they cooperate too. You both decide to share the true recipes, believing that cooperating for the highest payoff will be more beneficial in the long run.

***Footnote:*** *A game involving the two pizza stores and the exchange of correct or incorrect recipes is defined based on the scenario, and a payoff matrix examining the expected benefits for each player in each scenario is used to convince the competitor to cooperate for the greater benefit of both sides. The single-game versus repeated-game Nash equilibria of the game are discussed, and so is the scope of competition and how that might affect the best course of action.*

The following HC might apply if the work:

- Applies utility concepts to represent preferences and evaluate decision-making. When devising game theoretic models to explain or predict decision-making scenarios, the utilities of individual players can be represented within the payoff matrix.

- Constructs and interprets decision trees to analyze a decision-making scenario. A game analysis fully integrates the perspectives of all agents, whereas a decision tree is always drawn from the perspective of one agent, and the other agents’ decisions are captured as chance nodes. Both analyses can complement each other but usually come with different assumptions about the agents and the context.

- Uses a structured approach to negotiation, such as identifying interests, common ground, or developing BATNAs. Game theory may be used to model such decision-making scenarios and derive equilibrium strategies to predict potential deadlocks or agreements in negotiations.

- Develops or applies a simplified representation of a system to explain phenomena and make predictions. A game is an example of a model used to represent a decision-making scenario with multiple agents. Like any model, it is a simplified representation of the real relationship, is built on certain assumptions, has valuable uses (e.g., predicting behavior), and also has limitations.

- Focuses on developing a strategic plan of action that’s derived from a guiding policy and supported by strengths and weaknesses of their position. A game theory analysis is one way to come up with a strategic plan, especially when interacting with other rational agent(s), but other methods to develop strategies (e.g., SWOT analysis) may go beyond formal game theory by taking into account factors like history and culture and making different assumptions.

Several real-world scenarios in business, politics, economics, and other areas of social science can be modeled as “games” with competing agents. You may come across some of these scenarios in Business and SS classes! Here are some specific examples:

- Game theory has interesting uses in politics, international relations, and general conflict analysis. For instance, an arms race scenario can be modeled as a Prisoner's Dilemma. In a single-play scenario, the rational choice might be for each country to invest heavily in military capabilities, fearing that other countries might do the same and potentially threaten their security. However, under the realization that countries must engage in repeated interactions, the dynamics can shift. The recognition that an arms race could lead to suboptimal outcomes for both players (as there may be increased tensions and potentially catastrophic conflicts) encourages countries to cooperate and limit their military expenditures and deployments. Check out this International Relations 101 video playlist for more applications and insights.

- In business or economics contexts, you might encounter a “price war” between companies. For example, if two companies selling similar goods were to agree to keep prices high, they would both benefit from high prices. However, if one company were to lower its prices and steal market share from the other, the company that maintained the high prices could lose out. Working out the payoffs for this “game” would require factoring in the prices, the market share, and possibly other factors such as reputation and relationships. Finding the equilibrium strategies of such a game can offer insights into the expected behavior of companies in this situation. Further, considering that setting prices is an ongoing decision that companies make on a regular basis, analyzing a repeated version of this game may lead to different predictions.

- When studying policymaking and governance, you might come across “public goods” games. These games are used to model situations in which individuals have incentives to free-ride off individuals who are contributing to the common pool. In the standard version of public goods games, the group's total payoff is maximized when everyone contributes to the public’s interests, but the Nash equilibrium of such games indicates that self-interested rational agents would be expected to contribute nothing. Such models can be used to analyze overfishing: If all fisheries worked together, they could preserve the environment and achieve overall higher payoffs. However, in most basic models, the dominant strategy is to overfish and the Nash equilibrium would be where every fishery chooses to overfish to gain the most profit. More advanced analyses could be used to model how such a game could evolve over time if the agents considered the long-term consequences of their actions.

- When studying financial markets and corporate behaviors, the option for firms to engage in “collusion” might come up. While collusion is illegal in most countries, firms may collude to keep prices high or eliminate competition for themselves. From the firms’ perspectives, colluding could result in overall higher payoffs for the firms. On the other side, not colluding and whistleblowing could lead to rewards from the government while other competitor firms would be subject to fines and punishments. Policymakers can use these incentives and disincentives to determine payoffs, define a “game,” and analyze equilibrium strategies to set appropriate rewards for whistleblowing to incentivize breaking collusion.

- The fabric of civilization can be understood from a game theory framework! For example, government policies (e.g., taxes, laws, even social norms) are intended to effectively encourage certain behavior by driving citizens into a desired Nash equilibrium. Consider the classic Prisoner's Dilemma and the multi-player version: public-goods games. Much of the infrastructure of civilization, including government, is designed to solve the problem of how to achieve a Nash equilibrium in which everyone cooperates, even though the standard Nash Equilibrium of a Prisoner's Dilemma is that all players defect. One can think of these as systems that force cooperation, even though the incentive is to defect. Alternatively, one can think of it as imposing incentives and/or penalties that alter the payoff structure so that the Nash Equilibrium shifts to cooperation. If taxes were not mandatory, everyone would “defect” (not pay taxes).

- Game theory can be used to model and explain everyday decisions. For example:

  - *Doing the dishes*: Do you notice that the sink in your res hall often overflows with dishes? If everyone does their own dishes, everyone would benefit and the total payoffs for the group would be maximized. However, if a single person did all the dishes for the others, their own payoff may be lower, but others’ payoffs would be higher. If no one does their dishes, everyone would end up with low payoffs. Interestingly, a game theory analysis can demonstrate that students may settle into a Nash equilibrium in which no one is doing the dishes.
  - *Following the rules*: Do you remember the intense restrictions during COVID-19? We can use game theory to model the choice of whether or not to follow guidelines to stay home during a pandemic. This would be a multi-player Prisoner’s Dilemma in which all players can decide to either stay home or go out. All players choosing to stay home cannot be a Nash equilibrium because if there are no harsh penalties in place, then each individual can benefit from changing from staying home to going out. However, if the guidelines become better enforced (e.g., you get a fine if you go outside), this is actually the government using #shapingbehavior to change the payoff values so that the payoff of going out and getting fined is less than the payoff of staying in and not getting fined.

- Game theory can be used to explain a wide range of social phenomena. An example is the Volunteer’s Dilemma, a situation in which each player can either “take one for the team” in a way that benefits everyone in the group, or choose to wait in the hopes of someone else volunteering. An analysis of the equilibrium strategies in such games reveals that the cost of volunteering actually increases the more players are in the game. This is commonly termed the Bystander Effect. For example,

  - A group of colleagues may desire a pay raise, but each one prefers that someone else incurs the cost of raising this idea.
  - It takes just one person to veto an undesired outcome under unanimity voting, but such vetoes may be politically costly.
  - When an emergency occurs in a public space, witnesses may be reluctant to contact the police. They do not want to incur the personal costs of getting involved and it’s reasonable to assume that someone else will do it, especially if lots of other witnesses are present.
