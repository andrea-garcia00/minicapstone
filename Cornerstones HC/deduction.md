## #deduction

### Analyze and apply deductive reasoning.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

Reasoning toward indisputable conclusions.

Deduction involves reasoning to a logically certain conclusion on the basis of one or more propositions that serve as premises. This type of reasoning is a crucial aspect of logical thinking, whether it be for constructing arguments or carrying out mathematical derivations. Part of deduction involves analyzing relationships among propositions, for example by identifying logical inconsistencies, tautologies, and logical equivalence. Deductive arguments are evaluated on their validity (whether the premises entail the conclusion) and soundness (whether the premises are true).

**Big Question:** How is free choice manipulated?

You are wondering whether the bank is open today, a Saturday, because your little brother needs you to wire him money.

You know that on any given day, either the bank is closed for the whole day or the cafe next to it is closed for the whole day. You remember that last month you and your best friend were hanging out at the cafe and you discussed the basketball game that was scheduled for that evening. It was the championship game, which took place on a Saturday. Therefore, you conclude that the bank is not open on Saturdays.

You analyze that your argument is valid through a disjunctive syllogism: knowing that either the bank or the cafe is open on a day but not both, and knowing that the cafe was open on a Saturday allows you to validly infer that the bank was not open on Saturday. However, your deduction is only sound if all of your premises are true: it is possible, for instance, that the café you visited on a game day, on Saturday, was atypically open because of the game, and thus that the bank was actually also open on that (and normally all) Saturdays. You call the café to confirm, and they tell you that they do, in fact, open every Saturday, which in turn supports your premise (that either the bank or the café are open on a given day, but not both).

***Footnote:*** *Available knowledge is used to construct a deductive argument in the form of a disjunctive syllogism with an exclusive or. Let P = “bank is open on Saturday” and Q = “cafe is open on Saturday”, the argument reads: (P or Q) and not (P and Q); Q; therefore, not P. Since the argument is valid, the truth values of the premises are evaluated and confirmed to be true. Both the validity and soundness of the deductive reasoning are explained in detail and justified as necessary.*

- This HC is introduced in Formal Analyses using one particular formal logic system, sentential logic (SL), also called propositional logic. This system is based on logical statements and the relationships between them. However, there are many other formal logic systems that one can use, and all of them require the basic principles of #deduction! One example is quantified logic (QL), also called first-order logic or predicate logic. Depending on the situation, one system may be more useful than the other. SL and QL are explored in chapters 2 and 4 respectively of Magnus (2017), listed in the key resources. DeLancey (2017) covers the same material in Parts I and II of the text. For a list of other non-classical logical systems, check out this Wikipedia page.

- Deductive reasoning is essential for any course involving math (e.g., calculus, statistics, physics, chemistry, economics, business, etc.). When solving math problems, executing calculations, or performing proofs, deductive reasoning is essential. Mathematical axioms, rules, and theorems must be used correctly to derive each step, producing a result (conclusion) that is guaranteed to follow from the previous steps (premises).

  - An example is the definition of continuity. A function f(x) is said to be continuous at a point x = a, in its domain if these conditions are true:

  - f(a) exists

  - limx --&gt;a f(x) exists

  - limx --&gt;a f(x) = f(a)

  - Treating these conditions as premises, you can create an ‘argument’ where deductive reasoning is applied. For a function with these conditions, it is necessarily true that the function is continuous at a point x = a.

- This HC comes in handy in many areas of science given that the scientific method relies heavily on deductive reasoning. For example, we can use deductive reasoning to derive valid predictions and implications of a model, hypothesis, or theory. However, the predictions will only be true if the assumptions (premises) that the model or theory rests upon are true. Faulty assumptions can lead to problematic predictions (unsound argument). So, in these applications, the distinction between **validity** and **soundness** is really valuable for recognizing that the conclusions you draw from your model are only as good as its assumptions. Further, if observations or experimental results do not align with the predictions, we can use deductive reasoning to refute the hypothesis or identify problematic assumptions in our model.

- This HC is applied widely throughout the Philosophy, Ethics, and the Law core and concentration courses, both as a way to reconstruct arguments in resources you read and as a way to develop your own arguments. Students learn to pair deductive outlines with argument diagrams to improve their ability to visually distinguish premises from inferences in a deductive argument, identify multiple lines of reasoning in support of the same conclusion, make it easier to distinguish lines of reasoning from one another, and enable clear and precise objections to a deductive argument. Students practice two kinds of objections: objections to premises and objections to inferences.

- Unless you go into one of a very small number of niche academic fields, you may not be doing formal deductive proofs on a daily basis. However, this HC equips you with the ability to think carefully and critically about how to formalize something messy in the real world into a system that allows us to apply rigorous tools to solve the problem. For example, there is a **defensive** application, where you can use this HC to protect yourself from being overly swayed by arguments you encounter from, e.g., advertisements and politicians. There is also a **generative** application, where you can use this HC to sit down and look at everything you DO know (the premises), and you can think through what you can conclude from that information.

- There are several deductive strategies that we employ in our daily lives, such as “process of elimination” to arrive at the only viable conclusion, and using “counter-examples” to disprove a conclusion. For example, you might be figuring out who ate your leftover chicken from yesterday, among your flatmates, Tom, Chris, and Ashlyn. You know that Tom is a vegan, so he wouldn’t eat your chicken. You also know that Ashlyn is on a trip for a week, so she didn’t eat the chicken. Through eliminating Tom and Ashlyn, you conclude that Chris is the only one that could have possibly eaten the chicken. This argument is valid as the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises. Soundness might be questioned by examining the premises themselves. For example, if it turns out that Tom isn’t actually a vegan, then the accusation against Chris may not be justly supported. One single counter-example in which Tom was found eating non-vegan food would falsify that claim and make the argument unsound.

- Many professions make use of deductive reasoning: judges, mathematicians, physicists, neurologists, and CEOs. For example, for judges, there are relevant legal principles, statutes, regulations, and precedents that are set by previous court decisions. These serve as premises to reach a verdict. Judges can use deductive reasoning to connect these premises at hand and reach a conclusion.
