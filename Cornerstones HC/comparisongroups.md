## #comparisongroups

### Identify and evaluate appropriate comparison groups for empirical study designs.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

Without proper comparison groups, you might be comparing apples to oranges.

In empirical research, comparison groups are used to test alternative conditions or control for extraneous variables. These groups provide a comparison case for a result, giving the researcher more confidence that the independent variable of interest is having a measurable effect (or not) on the dependent variable. In most cases, one or more of the comparison groups should be a control group. Control groups are not subject to the independent variable or treatment of interest and are instead used as a baseline comparison and to identify the effect of extraneous variables (e.g., placebo treatment in a drug trial). Without an appropriate control group, results may be misleading or incomplete. Many studies use alternative comparison groups in addition to control groups. Comparison groups are also used when it is not possible to have a true control group. Alternative comparison groups test the effect of the independent variable in different ways (e.g., different doses of the same drug for one group of subjects vs. another or different levels of acidity in one part of the ocean compared to another). Choosing appropriate comparison groups can be difficult, therefore one must be able to identify the limitations of a particular comparison group and interpret the results accordingly. The goal is to create comparisons that evaluate the hypothesis while eliminating alternative interpretations.

**Big Question:** How do humans impact evolution?

You are designing a study to test a new teaching method in a middle school science class. The class is currently taught as a lecture with the teacher presenting new content to students using slides. Students can raise their hands to ask questions but are otherwise silent during lectures. You want to test the effect of active learning on students’ test scores. For one group you design a fully active class where students are engaged in discussion and practice with only a ten minute introductory lecture from the teacher. You also design a comparison group where there are 3-4 activities structured into the traditional lecture. Finally, you have to decide if you should have a control group that uses the traditional lecture format. Since many articles have been published showing that active learning improves student performance in class, you decide that the control group with no active learning may disadvantage students. You instead choose to use the test scores from the previous year’s lecture class as your control group and use the same test for your treatment groups.

***Footnote:*** *Comparison groups were set up to compare the effect of different levels of active learning (independent variable) on student test scores (dependent variable). A historical control group with no active learning was chosen instead of a concurrent control group since evidence pointed to a potentially negative effect on students.*

- Comparison groups used in medical studies are featured in Empirical Analyses. For example, if we are interested in the effect of a new drug on patients, the control group will receive placebo treatment (meaning taking pills that don’t have the drug), rather than not receiving anything at all. The placebo effect happens when a patient feels better about their health when receiving treatment, even though the treatment may have no positive effect.

- Comparison groups are also used in social studies. For example, suppose we want to study the effect of exercise on mental health. It would be unreasonable to compare an 18-year-old with an 80-year-old. Hence, considering age as a potential confounding variable, we can create comparison groups based on age. Then, we can compare the control (without exercise) and treatment (with exercise) groups of the same age intervals to mitigate this confounding effect. Of course, we would also have to carefully define what “counts” as exercise, etc. This is the creativity required in scientific research!

- The idea of comparison groups is useful day-to-day! Often there can be an instinct to compare one group to another without fully considering potential confounding variables. A shop owner might inadvertently think that a new marketing strategy was “effective” because their sales are higher. That said, for this to be true it would be critical to control for potential confounding variables. What if sales are higher simply because the economy is booming and people have more money to spend?

- Comparison groups are commonly used in academic research. For example, in evolutionary biology, when we examine human influences on the evolution of dogs’ brain sizes, we would have a group of dogs in the past (e.g. 50 years ago) and a group of dogs present. Further, we may want to split dogs based on their breeds (subspecies) as alternative comparison groups. Hence, if humans do influence dogs’ brain sizes, we expect similar changes across all subspecies. However, if there is one particular group that does not conform to the general trend (e.g., all subspecies have their brain sizes increasing, but Huskies’ brain sizes decline after 50 years), we may need to reconsider the original hypothesis.
