## #composition

### Communicate with a clear and precise style.

**Please fill out this Google Form to submit suggestions for this page (i.e. typos, study guide contributions, useful applications, etc.). We are actively seeking feedback on anything big or small to make the HC Handbook as useful as possible.**

Do not utilize the most complex and convoluted mechanisms of saying things; instead, communicate parsimoniously!

Effective communication requires a clear and precise style that reflects an authentic and genuine voice of the communicator while being appropriate for the intended audience. As a general helpful rule, communicators should use the fewest words necessary (they should respect the principle of “parsimony”) and should select words carefully to be as clear as possible and to facilitate transitions from one idea to the next. Strong composition requires avoiding passive voice, stilted diction, elaborate structure, and imprecision; write and speak simply, directly, and sincerely. Effectively applying these principles ensures we structure sentences to appropriately paraphrase, quote, or summarize information into our communications. Mastering these principles also involves knowing when to deliberately “break” a rule in order to convey intentional meanings to audiences.

**Big Question:** What might a perfect society look like?

You are visiting your aunt at her new house. She leaves a 5-minute long, convoluted voicemail about how to find her property, in which she tells you to drive on one road for “a while,” then turn “toward the property that just got sold by Kristin, who just had a child and is moving to New York” and take a right at the Millers’ farm. You have no way to know which property was recently sold or where the Millers live, and have no interest in knowing why Kristin sold her property. She then retracts some directions and suggests a detour around “the road with the Starbucks” to avoid construction. You realize en route that your cell phone’s battery is dead and you can no longer communicate with her, not knowing which of the five Starbucks in your proximity she meant. You try to follow her instructions, but quickly get lost. You wish her communications had been clear, precise, parsimonious and relevant.

***Footnote:*** *The aunt's messages exemplify a poor use of composition, as the messages are not clear and concise, using elaborate and indirect ways that could not be followed even by an attentive listener. It added irrelevant information and used diction that was not best suited for its purpose (“property” instead of “house” or “building”).*

- In business, you may investigate what a short tagline says about a brand. For example, you may compare the slogans: "We build a better world. Together.” with "Building a better world together." These slogans seem to have the same meaning but are composed differently. The first slogan includes a period between “world” and “together,” which seems to emphasize the importance of “together.” In contrast, the second slogan has no full stops, which gives the impression that all the words are equally important. Both of these slogans seem to lack detail because the intended audience would need significant background information to understand how the company plans to build a better world with them. By analyzing the #composition of the slogan options, one can suggest improvements in the clarity and precision of the phrase.

- In science, you may use #composition to ensure you communicate your findings in the most parsimonious way. The details of the scientific study may be complex; however, when writing your report, you try to explain the mechanisms and results in the simplest way possible, allowing your audience to know more than just what your results were but to grasp why and how you found those results. To do this, you follow the principle of parsimony, avoid run-on sentences, and reduce your use of the passive voice. Your writing becomes more efficient now that you have prioritized your audience’s understanding and compositional choices. You tag #composition to justify why you made those choices and how they elevated your work.

- You can use #composition in your daily communications too! Many people spend time each day sending texts. However, not many people think about how to compose such messages so they are understandable. Taking the extra second to ask yourself if the message you are about to send could be misinterpreted may save you from conflicts. Of course, your texts do not need to be the most parsimonious and beautifully crafted writing. However, avoiding ambiguity and confusion would still be helpful. People tend to avoid punctuation or only use it for emphasis, making texting a hard place to navigate compositionally. However, sometimes punctuation changes the meaning of a sentence, e.g. “did you eat, grandma?” vs. “did you eat grandma?” One sentence asks a simple question, and one implies cannibalism! Apply #composition to your casual writing whenever possible.

  - Written in 1946, Orwell’s famous essay laments the decline of the English language due to the laziness and bad habits of its speakers. He outlines the bad habits he wishes to see eliminated and explains how lazy writing both leads to and is a result of lazy thinking. Orwell believes that these bad habits not only limit language, they also contribute to political and cultural decline. Although most of Orwell’s sample words and phrases are no longer current, one can apply his ideas to today’s writing in order to improve communication.
